Lift and stall speed

A wing makes lift by turning air downward. How much depends on how fast it moves through the air, how big it is, and how hard it works (its lift coefficient).

Lift

L = ½ · ρ · V² · S · CL

ρ air density (1.225 kg/m³), V airspeed (m/s), S wing area (m²), CL lift coefficient. It rises with angle of attack until the wing stalls. Double the speed and you get four times the lift.

Level flight

L = W = m · g
V = √( 2W / (ρ · S · CL) )

To hold altitude, lift equals weight. At any chosen CL that fixes the speed you must fly.

Stall speed

V_stall = √( 2W / (ρ · S · CLmax) )

CLmax is the most lift the wing can make before the airflow separates. For a tiltrotor, stall speed is the speed the transition has to reach before the rotors stop carrying the weight.

Wing loading

loading = m / S   [grams per dm²]

Mass per wing area. Low loading is floaty and slow. High loading is fast, penetrates wind, and gives less margin in the transition.

Where a curve crosses the weight line is the speed at which the wing alone can carry the aircraft. The green curve crossing is your stall speed.

Drag, aspect ratio and cruise power

Drag has two parts that trade off against speed. Slow flight needs a high CL, which costs induced drag. Fast flight pushes the whole airframe through the air, which costs parasitic drag. The sweet spot sits between.

Aspect ratio

AR = b² / S   (= b / c if rectangular)

Long and thin (AR 8 to 10) is efficient. Short and wide (AR 3 to 4) is stiff and stalls gently but pays in induced drag. A VTOL that cruises briefly can accept a low AR.

Drag coefficient

CDi = CL² / (π · e · AR)
CD  = CD0 + CDi

CDi is induced drag, the cost of making lift. CD0 is parasitic drag from skin, nacelles, gaps and struts. e (0.6 to 0.9) says how close the wing is to ideal.

Drag force and L/D

D   = ½ · ρ · V² · S · CD
L/D = CL / CD

L/D is how many grams of lift you get per gram of drag. A glide covers L/D times its height in distance.

Power and current

P_aero = D · V
P_elec = P_aero / η
I      = P_elec / V_batt

η bundles motor, ESC and prop efficiency (0.4 to 0.6 for small props). V_batt is about 3.7 V per cell under load.

The dip in the curve is the speed that needs the least power, so it gives the longest flight time. Below it, induced drag climbs. Above it, parasitic drag climbs. Flying faster than the dip is fine, it just costs more.

Thrust, hover and battery

In hover the rotors are the wing. Everything here comes from how much thrust three small props make, and how much power that takes.

Thrust-to-weight

T/W = (n · T_motor) / W

2 is the bare minimum for a VTOL. 2.5 or more leaves room for control corrections, gusts and the transition.

Throttle and thrust

thrust ∝ throttle²
hover throttle ≈ √(W / T_total)
thrust ∝ V_batt²

Because thrust goes with the square, half your thrust needs about 70% throttle. Going from 3S to 4S gives about 1.8 times the thrust on the same prop.

Momentum theory

P_ideal = T^1.5 / √(2 · ρ · A_disc)
P_hover = P_ideal / FM
disc loading = W / A_disc

Ideal hover power drops as total disc area grows, so bigger props hover more cheaply. The figure of merit FM (0.4 to 0.6 for small props) covers real losses.

Battery

energy (Wh)  = V_batt · Ah
time (min)   = 60 · capacity · usable / I
I_max (A)    = C · capacity (Ah)

Use about 80% of a LiPo so you never fly it flat. The C rating limits the current you can draw, and voltage sags as you pull it.

Prop pitch speed

RPM     ≈ KV · V_batt · 0.85
V_pitch = RPM · pitch

A prop only pushes while the aircraft moves slower than its pitch speed. You want pitch speed at least 1.5 times your cruise speed.

Thrust per motor, cell count, capacity and prop diameter come from Your aircraft. Hover assumes the three rotors share load equally. The real split depends on the CG (see Rotor layout). Real flights land about 20% below this time because of sag and corrections.

Center of gravity and stability

A plane is pitch-stable when the CG sits ahead of the neutral point, the spot where the whole aircraft's lift effectively acts. The gap between them is the static margin.

Center of gravity

x_cg = Σ( m_i · x_i ) / Σ m_i

The mass-weighted average position of every part, measured from one fixed reference (here the wing leading edge, positive aft). Moving a heavy part far from the CG shifts it a lot, so trim with the battery.

Static margin

SM = (x_np − x_cg) / c

Distance between neutral point and CG, as a fraction of the chord. Negative is unstable. 5 to 15% is the usual target. Too large and it gets nose-heavy.

Tail volume

V_H = S_h · l_t / (S · c)
V_V = S_v · l_t / (S · b)
V-tail:  S_h = S_tail · cos²Γ
         S_v = S_tail · sin²Γ

Tail area times its lever arm, relative to the wing. Bigger or farther tail means more stability. Γ is the angle of each tail panel from horizontal.

Neutral point (rough)

a    = 2π / (1 + 2/(e·AR))
dε/dα = 2a / (π · AR)
h_np = 0.25 + (a_t/a)(1 − dε/dα)·V_H·0.9

A bare wing's neutral point is at 25% chord. The tail pushes it aft by an amount set by tail volume, less the wing's downwash on the tail. This is a hand estimate. Check it in XFLR5 before trusting it.

Rotor layout and hover balance

In hover there is no wing, so the three motors balance the aircraft like a seesaw around the CG. The geometry decides how much each motor has to push.

Force balance

T_front_total + T_rear = W

The three thrusts together equal the weight. The two front motors count as one combined thrust.

Moment balance

T_front_total · d_front = T_rear · d_rear

Thrust times arm length, measured from the CG, must cancel front against rear. Solving both equations gives each motor's share:

T_rear        = W · d_f / (d_f + d_r)
T_front_total = W · d_r / (d_f + d_r)

Clearances

axis ahead of LE = d_front − x_cg
prop-to-wing gap  = that − r
prop-to-prop gap  = 2·y − 2r
prop-to-body gap  = y − r − w/2

r is the prop radius, y the nacelle's distance from the centerline, w the fuselage width. Positive gaps mean no contact. Leave at least 20 mm to the wing and 10 mm elsewhere.

Control surfaces, transition and ArduPilot

The flight controller mixes your stick inputs into motor speeds, tilt angles and surface movements. What each stick does depends on which phase you are in.

In hover (rotors do the work)

Roll: the two front motors push unequally.

Pitch: front motors against the rear motor.

Yaw: the front nacelles tilt in opposite directions.

Altitude: all three motors together.

In cruise (wing does the work)

Roll: ailerons.

Pitch: both V-tail surfaces move the same way.

Yaw: V-tail surfaces move opposite ways (a ruddervator mix).

Speed: the two front motors; the rear motor is off.

Parts of the autopilot

IMU: gyro and accelerometer inside the flight controller. They measure rotation and acceleration thousands of times a second.

PID: the control loop that turns attitude error into corrections. Tuning sets how firm and how smooth it feels.

SITL: a simulator that runs the real ArduPilot code, so you can test before risking hardware.

  1. 1. HoverRotors vertical. All three motors carry the weight.
  2. 2. Tilt forwardFront nacelles rotate and the aircraft accelerates.
  3. 3. Wing takes overOnce airspeed passes stall speed, lift carries the weight. The rear motor winds down.
  4. 4. CruiseFlying as a plane on the front motors and control surfaces.

Landing runs this in reverse. The ArduPilot name for this kind of aircraft is a QuadPlane. Typical modes are QSTABILIZE, QHOVER and QLOITER (rotor flight), FBWA (assisted wing flight) and RTL (return to launch). Parameter names start with Q_, for example the tilt settings. Check exact names and ranges in the ArduPilot documentation for the firmware version you install.
