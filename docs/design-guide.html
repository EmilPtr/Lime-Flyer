Lift and stall speed

A wing makes lift by turning air downward. How much depends on how fast it moves through the air, how big it is, and how hard it works (its lift coefficient).

Lift

L = ½ · ρ · V² · S · CL

ρ air density (1.225 kg/m³), V airspeed (m/s), S wing area (m²), CL lift coefficient. It rises with angle of attack until the wing stalls. Double the speed and you get four times the lift.

Level flight

L = W = m · g
V = √( 2W / (ρ · S · CL) )

To hold altitude, lift equals weight. At any chosen CL that fixes the speed you must fly.

Stall speed

V_stall = √( 2W / (ρ · S · CLmax) )

CLmax is the most lift the wing can make before the airflow separates. Stall speed sets how fast you must throw the plane at launch and how fast it lands. Slower is gentler on a foam wing.

Wing loading

loading = m / S   [grams per dm²]

Mass per wing area. Low loading is floaty and slow, which suits a micro plane. High loading is fast and handles wind better, but lands hard.

Drag, aspect ratio and cruise power

Drag has two parts that trade off against speed. Slow flight needs a high CL, which costs induced drag. Fast flight pushes the whole airframe through the air, which costs parasitic drag. The sweet spot sits between.

Aspect ratio

AR = b² / S   (= b / c if rectangular)

Long and thin (AR 8 to 10) is efficient. Short and wide (AR 3 to 4) is stiff and stalls gently but pays in induced drag. A small foam trainer sits around AR 5 to 7. Longer is more efficient, but shorter is stiffer and survives crashes better.

Drag coefficient

CDi = CL² / (π · e · AR)
CD  = CD0 + CDi

CDi is induced drag, the cost of making lift. CD0 is parasitic drag from skin, the fuselage, tail, gaps and exposed servos. e (0.6 to 0.9) says how close the wing is to ideal.

Drag force and L/D

D   = ½ · ρ · V² · S · CD
L/D = CL / CD

L/D is how many grams of lift you get per gram of drag. A glide covers L/D times its height in distance.

Power and current

P_aero = D · V
P_elec = P_aero / η
I      = P_elec / V_batt

η bundles motor, ESC and prop efficiency (0.4 to 0.6 for small props). V_batt is about 3.7 V per cell under load.

The dip in the curve is the speed that needs the least power, so it gives the longest flight time. Below it, induced drag climbs. Above it, parasitic drag climbs. Flying faster than the dip is fine, it just costs more.

Thrust and battery

A fixed-wing needs far less thrust than a hover aircraft: enough to launch, climb and hold cruise, with a margin. The battery sets how long you stay up.

Thrust-to-weight

T/W = T_motor / W

Static thrust divided by weight. Above 1 it can climb. 1.5 or more launches and recovers comfortably. Aim for about 2 on a hand-launched micro plane.

Throttle and thrust

thrust ∝ throttle²
cruise throttle ≈ √(D / T_motor)
thrust ∝ V_batt²

Because thrust goes with the square, half your thrust needs about 70% throttle. Adding one cell to a 2S pack gives about 2.25 times the thrust on the same prop. The cruise throttle here is a rough guess that ignores airspeed.

Battery

energy (Wh)  = V_batt · Ah
time (min)   = 60 · capacity · usable / I
I_max (A)    = C · capacity (Ah)

Use about 80% of a LiPo so you never fly it flat. The C rating limits the current you can draw, and voltage sags as you pull it.

Prop pitch speed

RPM     ≈ KV · V_batt · 0.85
V_pitch = RPM · pitch

A prop only pushes while the aircraft moves slower than its pitch speed. You want pitch speed at least 1.5 times your cruise speed.

Thrust, cell count, capacity and cruise speed come from the sections above. Flight time assumes steady cruise. Climbs, turns and cold batteries cut it, so real flights land about 30% below this.

Center of gravity and stability

A plane is pitch-stable when the CG sits ahead of the neutral point, the spot where the whole aircraft's lift effectively acts. The gap between them is the static margin. The tail is what moves the neutral point aft.

Center of gravity

x_cg = Σ( m_i · x_i ) / Σ m_i

The mass-weighted average position of every part, measured from one fixed reference (here the wing leading edge, positive aft). Moving a heavy part far from the CG shifts it a lot, so trim with the battery.

Static margin

SM = (x_np − x_cg) / c

Distance between neutral point and CG, as a fraction of the chord. Negative is unstable. 5 to 15% is the usual target. Too large and it gets nose-heavy.

Tail volume

V_H = S_h · l_t / (S · c)
V_V = S_v · l_t / (S · b)

Tail area times its lever arm, relative to the wing. S_h is the stabilizer (with elevator), S_v the fin. Bigger or farther tail means more stability.

Neutral point (rough)

a    = 2π / (1 + 2/(e·AR))
dε/dα = 2a / (π · AR)
h_np = 0.25 + (a_t/a)(1 − dε/dα)·V_H·0.9

A bare wing's neutral point is at 25% chord. The tail pushes it aft by an amount set by tail volume, less the wing's downwash on the tail. This is a hand estimate. Check it with a glide test.

Control surfaces and radio setup

Three control surfaces plus the throttle fly a conventional plane. The FS-i6 sends each stick straight to its own channel, so there is nothing to mix.

What each input does

Roll: the two ailerons move opposite ways.

Pitch: the elevator on the stabilizer. Up elevator pitches the nose up.

Yaw: a rudder on the fin, if you fit one. Without it, bank to turn.

Speed: throttle on the single motor.

Radio setup

Ailerons: plug both servos into one channel with a Y-lead. Mount them mirror-image so they move opposite ways, or reverse one with a servo reverser.

Directions: check with the props off. Stick right should raise the right aileron and drop the left. Stick back should raise the elevator.

Throws: start small and add rates only after the first glide.

Before launch

Range check: walk the transmitter away with the props off.

Failsafe: set throttle to cut and controls to neutral if the link drops.

Balance: weigh and balance on the CG mark with the battery installed.

  1. 1. Glide testNo motor power. Throw the plane level over soft ground and watch the glide.
  2. 2. TrimFix nose-up or nose-down tendencies with the elevator trim or the battery position.
  3. 3. Powered launchHand launch into the wind at about half throttle, level or a little nose-up.
  4. 4. Cruise and landFly at the efficient speed from the Drag section, then glide in with the throttle off.

Keep early flights short and low, near a soft landing area. A crash in foam is usually a glue job, not a rebuild.
